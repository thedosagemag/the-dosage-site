Born and bred in music

The sound that is Ryan De Mel. The 21-year-old singer on Voiceprint, his trademark cover, and why music heals.

The DosageFirst published December 2015
▶ Watch

From the stirring rendition of Marvin Gaye’s hit single “Sexual Healing,” which has become his trademark cover, to spontaneously rapping out his mother’s grocery list, 21-year-old Ryan De Mel is one of Sri Lanka’s fast-rising musicians. Both a solo artist and a member of the local band Voiceprint, Ryan has made a niche for himself in the island’s music scene as a strong competitor who is here to stay for years to come. Dosage sat down with this budding star, whose personality is as much a compliment to his incredible talent as it is to the magnanimous human being behind the music.

What’s new with you, musically?

A year ago I joined Voiceprint. It’s taken a lot of my time and been keeping me busy. I’m also working on a solo project. I’m doing some covers and I’ll be releasing them individually on my YouTube channel sometime soon.

You’ve been singing since you were a child. How did it all begin?

I’d say I could sing before I could talk. Both my parents come from a musical background and they both sing, so I was born and bred in music. I wasn’t forced into it; it was just something I wanted to do. When I was ten, my mom enrolled me in professional voice training with Marianne David and I’m still continuing to train.

What do you think about your progression from a solo singer to a group singer?

I’ve actually been a group singer from a small age. I sang for the choir, and even though we sang as a group we were trained as soloists. So I wouldn’t say I’ve progressed, but I do see a progression in the genres I choose to sing. Switching from classical to contemporary was hard because they have different techniques. But once I got into it, I was able to go from acoustic to classical and vice versa.

What are your biggest influences, and how has music affected your personality?

My first influence would be my parents. Once I got into music, the artists I follow, like Michael Bublé, Pharrell and Chris Brown, became a huge influence. Music is pretty much my personality. And the artists I like are pretty versatile, so I find influence in jazz, pop and R&B.

You got pretty famous for your cover of Marvin Gaye’s “Sexual Healing.” What’s the story behind it?

Ha! You know how most songs have some deep meaning behind their origin? This one was pretty straightforward. I was practising for a gig with a friend and we wanted to do something that wasn’t so mainstream. When we narrowed it down, we decided on this song. It really paid off and became this huge hit. Now I have to perform it at every gig.

Is it safe to say that music is healing?

Definitely. There are some illnesses doctors can’t figure out, but music can heal. When you’re feeling a little down or lonely, you don’t really need medication. You need music; you need lyrics you can connect with. You’d be amazed how the right song can change your mood at the snap of a finger.

When you’re feeling a little down or lonely, you don’t really need medication. You need music.

What’s one of the funniest things that has happened to you onstage?

(Laughs) I have so many funny stories, but one stands out. I was doing a gig at Gateway, singing “Sexual Healing” (a song I’ve sung a million times) when all of a sudden I blanked on the second word. So I spontaneously rapped out my mom’s grocery list. And the best part was that no one noticed. They were so into it, I was so into it, it didn’t matter that I was rapping about tomatoes, milk and carrots.

The psychology of music

How music moves us, reflects our moods, and sometimes says what people cannot.

The DosageFirst published December 2015

Music as an expression, as an art or simply as a way of life, is therapeutic to all who behold it, in its simplicity as well as its complexity. It’s a sense of belonging, or a longing to belong, whichever we are seeking, that connects us to music.

Music undoubtedly affects human emotion. From the classical tones of Bach and Beethoven to contemporary independent genres or the heavier tones of rock, metal or grunge, we tend to listen to what best reflects our moods.

Many people cite music as a route out of distressing times in their lives. And so it is. Music not only reflects but affects temperament, and may help alleviate low moods.

An almost telepathic connection that music has with its listeners is its ability to communicate feeling. Music has emotional components in its nuances that can reflect and draw out sensations ranging from deep, moving sorrow to inexplicable elation, to unsettling fear and even irrepressible anger. Cinema has used this for aeons to move its audiences. It is also the element that makes our hairs stand on end and our hearts flicker with delicious emotion. Music moves. It can move one to tears. This power can thaw almost any cold disposition, and by that argument music can alleviate anger and depression, or help someone see things from your perspective when the need arises.

Sometimes music can speak human emotion in a way that humans cannot.

In a most clichéd retelling of a classic tale, I have had many a friend (and a personal experience) who, unable to express herself to a significant other, sent him a song she had recently heard. After months of silent misunderstanding, neither had been able to communicate their deep-seated needs to the other as that song did. This is something we can all relate to. Whether we choose to share that song or not, it is undisputed that sometimes music can speak human emotion in a way that humans cannot.

From the seven-year-old girl who woke to an Adele song after a week in a coma following a freak accident, to Sam Carter, aged 60, who was given a 30% chance of waking from a coma after severe anaemia but awoke to the Rolling Stones’ “(I Can’t Get No) Satisfaction,” music has proven through the years that its healing powers transcend the conscious.

From going to sleep with Simon and Garfunkel in your head to waking up six hours later with the same song, it is music that defines our very existence. Psychology and physiology are made that much more magical by our relationship with, and response to, the therapeutic phenomenon with a universal language and communicating power that is music.

CrossFit with Andy Andrews

Certified the fittest in Sri Lanka in the 2015 Reebok CrossFit Games Open, the golfer-turned-trainer explains the sport of fitness.

The DosageFirst published December 2015
▶ Watch

Andy Andrews is an internationally qualified CrossFit trainer currently working at Vaaj Fitness Centre, and a professional golfer who aims to play a vital role in the fitness revolution in Sri Lanka. Andy was certified as the fittest in Sri Lanka in the 2015 Reebok CrossFit Games Open.

What is the story behind CrossFit, and what motivated you to be part of it?

I have played golf all my life, and strength and conditioning is a very important factor in becoming a successful golfer. I came to Sri Lanka a few months ago looking for a good fitness centre to work out at. That’s when I came across Duminda, and he asked me to fill in for some classes. It grew from there, and I still haven’t left. Sri Lanka has a huge gap in developing healthy individuals into healthy athletes, and since our lifestyles and food patterns have changed, people pay less attention to physical fitness and are more prone to conditions like high cholesterol and high blood pressure.

CrossFit at Vaaj: what is special here?

It is the only one in Sri Lanka. A lot of people claim to do it, but we are the only licensed ones.

Are your clients genuinely conscious about their health, or do they want to be part of a trend?

Good question! A lot of people come and tell us they want to lose weight or get their stomachs smaller. CrossFit is a performance-based training programme: we look at what your body can do, not what it looks like. Both people who are genuinely health-conscious and people who want to be part of a trend come, but what matters most is that you remain fit and healthy.

CrossFit is the sport of fitness.

We were told CrossFit is a sport. What do you have to say about that?

Initially, CrossFit was just a training programme. Generally, people go to the gym and spend hours working out, which is totally unnecessary. The element of time was introduced to CrossFit, and that changed things: you had to complete the tasks as fast as possible, or do as many as you could in a given time. That made it competitive and created the hunger to perform better. The first CrossFit Games were held in 2007, and now it is a multi-million-dollar event held every July in California. What’s special is that the workouts are ones anybody with a foundation level of fitness could do.

A physiotherapist’s view

Asanga Jayalath, physiotherapist

What do you think of CrossFit and the exercises involved, from a physiotherapist’s perspective?

This programme strengthens muscle through activity; the important aspect is that the movements are not isolated. With the rise in non-communicable diseases, people need to bring exercise into their lives. CrossFit is one way of improving fitness and health, and it helps increase the mobility, strength and coordination of one’s body.

If CrossFit is a sport, and all sports involve injuries, what kinds of injuries do you see?

CrossFit is for all ages, so instructors should be especially responsible when training kids. Children in the 13–14 age group are still developing their muscles, so heavy weights can cause ligament and tendon tears. Instructors should know the limits.

Middle-aged women often join these programmes to keep fit and lose belly fat. What’s your advice to them?

Women, especially after menopause, should know that oestrogen is a vital hormone in strengthening bones, and after menopause its secretion drops, which can lead to osteoporosis or osteoarthritis. These conditions can cause bone damage during strenuous exercise, so it’s important to get proper advice from medical professionals and your instructors, and to know your limits.

Injuries are inevitable, but ladies love to squat?

Squatting helps build well-toned back muscles, but women, especially those carrying belly fat or recovering from surgery or childbirth, should brief their instructors before signing up, because weightlifting puts pressure on ligaments and rotator cuffs and can cause injuries. Lifting can even put pressure on the bladder or uterus, which may prolapse and cause bleeding, which can be very dangerous.

“As a physiotherapist, what I suggest is that you get proper professional advice and educate your personal instructor about your body, so they know exactly what you need and can train you without injury.”

The Gym Review: Vaaj Fitness Centre

Inside the gym that wanted to be a full fitness centre: assessments, a sports doctor, a dietician, and a CEO with a system.

The DosageFirst published December 2015
▶ Watch

Mothilal Jayathilake

CEO, Vaaj Fitness Centre · Rugby coach and trainer

What’s your clientele like here?

We have close to 500 members.

As a strength and conditioning instructor, which groups do you train, and are there specific programmes for each?

We cater mostly to people doing strength and conditioning for sport. The problem in Sri Lanka is that people don’t know the difference between fitness training and sport training.

The problem here in Sri Lanka is that people don’t know the difference between fitness training and sport training.

Take rugby players. How does their training differ from a normal person’s?

Sports demand many physical qualities. In rugby you need to be bigger, stronger and faster, which means strength, power and endurance. In tennis you need agility and speed. So training differs from sport to sport. But you need basic training from childhood, like periodisation plans, so you can plan your training.

What is your opinion on supplements?

Internationally, in countries like the USA and UK, athletes spend a lot on supplements because high-intensity training requires the correct proteins, vitamins and carbohydrates in the right quantities. The food we find in Sri Lanka often doesn’t have sufficient nutrient content, so you need supplements.

For someone like me (Darshan), would you recommend supplements?

Yes. First training, then nutrition, then supplements.

What would you like to say about Vaaj from your experience so far?

Here we have everything under one roof, and a system I introduced. When you become a member, we start with a fitness consultation and a fitness assessment. Then we do a health screening and tell you where you stand. For the first six weeks we normally work on balance and posture, because in Sri Lanka we need a lot of balance and posture correction, which is a type of functional screening. After another six weeks we test again to identify your strengths and weaknesses, then work on maintaining the strengths and brushing away the weaknesses.

Duminda de Silva

Personal trainer and director

You changed the name from “Glory” to “Vaaj.” What’s the story?

When we first started Glory we had healthy demand, and we wanted to expand as much as we could. Along the way we met Mr. Vaaj, who became one of our main investors, so we gave his brand name priority.

What motivated you to become a fitness instructor?

At school I was a sportsman who trained a lot to maintain fitness and stamina. After my professional sports career ended, I saw that many gyms in Sri Lanka didn’t have well-trained, qualified people to cater to their members. So I got qualified internationally and started my own gym.

What is the concept behind Vaaj?

Vaaj is a one-stop place for healthy living and fitness, unlike any other gym, because it is a fitness centre. From the day you join, we do a fitness assessment and give you a schedule. A doctor specialising in sports medicine does a health assessment, and a dietician recommends a diet programme. After a period of training, we look at where you have improved and where you should improve further.

Do people come because they are genuinely fitness-conscious, or to be part of a trend?

Both, to be honest.

So what is different at Vaaj?

Having trained people in many different countries, I can say this place gives you the total solution: periodic reviews, a doctor’s assessment and a dietician’s advice. In Sri Lanka, people usually start working out on their first day at the gym without any fitness assessment, which we conduct here.

Mr. Waruna

CrossFit client at Vaaj Fitness Centre

What do you think about CrossFit here?

I got into CrossFit a few months back. It’s different from weight training, which is monotonous. CrossFit combines gymnastics and powerlifting against the clock. I love the challenge and try to push my limits every day. Long term, I want to get into the CrossFit Games.

How has the process been since the beginning?

At the start I found it difficult because it is strenuous, but as you go on you see the improvement in stamina and strength. I think I have improved a lot, and I have seen the results.

How often do you train, and what do you do?

About three days a week, though I plan to increase that. Sessions run from half an hour to about 45 minutes, mostly bodyweight training and powerlifting. I try to eat healthy food, with more protein and fewer carbs. At the end of the week, my goal is simple: I want to get fitter.

The medical team

Nirosha Gallage, dietician · Dr. Thushantha Wijesinghe, sports medicine consultant

How does the medical side work at Vaaj?

At Mr. Duminda De Silva’s request, we screen clients when they join, checking for issues in the cardiovascular, locomotor and respiratory systems before any exercise, and we also look at injuries such as musculoskeletal ones. We assess fat percentage and body mass index (BMI).

When a client joins, what red flags do you look for?

We first look at their history for non-communicable diseases such as diabetes and hypertension, and at their social and family history to understand their lifestyle. Then we examine the cardiovascular, locomotor and respiratory systems and give the client a training plan that suits them best.

What happens if a client is injured during a routine?

Instructors are trained to manage the client at the time of injury. The doctor then reassesses the client and decides whether further tests, like an MRI or CT scan, are needed. If physiotherapy is required, the client is referred to the physiotherapist, who refers back to the doctor.

Is there a magic formula for weight loss?

There are no shortcuts. It takes a lot of time. You should not undergo a dramatic drop in weight because it affects your health badly. One way to maintain your weight is to eat on time and have a balanced diet.

What is your opinion on supplements, and are they dangerous?

Supplements are useful, but they are meant for bodybuilders and weightlifters, not ordinary gym-goers, and people should seek professional advice on what, how much and which brands. Everything has pros and cons; it depends how you use them. Overconsumption can damage the kidneys. For athletes, we recommend supplements and nutrition plans depending on their events, with hydration and carbohydrate intake the two main things we consider.

Muscle Motivation, Episode 01

Bodybuilder Harintha Tillekeratne on gaining his first 15 kilos, supplements and consistency. Plus four towel exercises with trainer Shawn Roache.

The DosageFirst published December 2015
▶ Watch

When you started bodybuilding, did you set any targets?

I started going to the gym because I had to row for my college. I was 55 kilos at the time, which wasn’t a good weight class for rowing, and I had to gain 10 to 15 kilos. Within a couple of months I saw results.

Do competitive bodybuilders do it to look good, or are there other motivations?

For bodybuilders, bodybuilding is their life, just like it is for me. It makes you look good, but that is not the ultimate goal. The goal is to achieve something more than normal: to build mass and burn fat at the same time.

Do you use supplements?

When I started I took protein supplements. After my rowing career ended in 2012, I took whey protein and went on to creatine. Multivitamins and fish oils are also essential.

Is it hard to maintain what you have achieved?

Maintenance is the hardest part. You can gain muscle easily, but consistency is very challenging.

Maintenance is the hardest part. You can gain muscle easily, but consistency is very challenging.

How has bodybuilding affected your self-image and relationships?

A lot of people say there is a bit of intimidation around me. As for relationships, it depends on the person, and for me it is going all right.

At what age should a youngster start weightlifting and bodybuilding?

Lifting weights, ideally around 16. Bodybuilding takes you to a rougher side, with supplements, a controlled diet and heavier weights, so I would advise starting after 18.

What have you got to say to the youngsters out there?

Bodybuilding moulds you into a person who doesn’t care about others’ opinions. Training is vital. Try not to miss or cut any sessions, be honest with yourself, and you will see a change.

Workout with Shawn

Personal trainer Shawn Roache gives you easy-to-do exercises that help you stay fit

Overhead squat with towel. Stand with your feet slightly wider than shoulder-width apart, toes turned out slightly. Grab a rolled-up towel with an overhand grip, hands shoulder-width apart, and raise it overhead so your shoulders are roughly in line with your heels, elbows slightly bent. Squat as far as possible without letting your knees jut past your toes, then return to standing. Keep your shoulders stacked (draw your shoulder blades down). Works your quadriceps, hamstrings, calves, core and deltoids; the towel keeps your shoulders aligned.

Towel sit-up. Hold the towel between your hands about shoulder-width apart and keep as much tension on it as possible throughout. Lift your head and chest off the floor; the more you push your chest out, the more effective it is. Start with your arms fully extended, then pull the towel back towards your chest and out to full extension again. Keep your glutes firm. Focuses on your back and chest.

Hip hinge to trunk extension. Not to be confused with a squat. Start with your hips hinged backwards and abdominals engaged; your lower back should be adequately arched and your neck not overly extended. Arms extend slightly beyond the trunk, feet flat. In the final phase, explode upwards, raising your arms overhead with hips and knees extended, weight on the balls of your feet. Keep your abdominals tight so you don’t over-arch your lower back. Works your core, glutes, quadriceps, hamstrings and calves.

Occupational therapy for you

At the Ragama Rehabilitation Hospital, therapists use sport, art, music and drama to help patients rebuild their lives.

The DosageFirst published December 2015
▶ Watch

Occupational therapy is a branch of medicine in which therapists and their assistants help people through three phases of physical and mental learning: building, recovering and maintaining. Common interventions help children and adults do activities that would otherwise be beyond them, or regain motor and cognitive skills lost to physical or psychological damage. It doesn’t give anyone superhuman abilities; it helps them reach the potential that exists or, more commonly, has been lost.

Joining us for this discussion was Mr. P.D. Yapa, a veteran of occupational therapy with over 35 years of experience, and secretary of the Sri Lanka Occupational Therapy Association. Under his guidance, many have overcome life-changing trauma.

We help them regain the motor and cognitive skills lost due to physical or psychological damage. Mr. P.D. Yapa

What does the therapy process involve?

At the Ragama occupational therapy unit, where Mr. Yapa worked, each patient is evaluated individually and the therapist sets immediate goals. Customised interventions then improve the patient’s ability to do everyday activities, and the therapist checks that the goals are met.

After a patient is discharged, how important is follow-up?

“Following up is very important because it lets us see the patient’s improvement. But because we lack therapists, home visits to observe progress are difficult, and most of the time there are fewer funds available than we need for follow-ups.”

Mr. Yapa recently published a book on occupational therapy for the general public. It covers conditions such as strokes, arthritis, spinal injuries and geriatric care, and was written to help people manage these situations at home and in their communities.

Occupational therapy units exist in most major hospitals in Sri Lanka. Most psychiatric disorders are treated at Angoda, while physical disorders are treated in most hospitals. Mr. Yapa believes occupational therapy isn’t restricted to hospitals: its principles apply to workplaces, schools and training institutes, wherever someone can be helped to improve their quality of life.

Inside Ragama

While visiting the centre we met Mr. Jayaweera, chief occupational therapist at the rehabilitation hospital in Ragama. “As occupational therapists, we use specially selected activities to give patients the opportunity to regain their lost abilities. These include physical exercise, sport, music, art and drama.”

Therapists here help people affected by trauma, illness or disability through movement and exercise, manual therapy, training and advice, and they offer psychological support so patients can stay in employment and remain independent for as long as possible. They work, for example, with soldiers disabled in the war, helping them live daily life with a degree of normality while preparing them psychologically to return to society.

Some traumatic incidents cause partial or total loss of speech. Ms. Kalyani Madawala, a speech and language therapist at the hospital, works hand in hand with the occupational therapy department to help adults and children with cognitive and physical disabilities improve their language and communication. Treatments are tailored by age: children, for example, might be trained using toys.

We also met children with autism and Down syndrome being helped through interactive therapy, free of drugs and the discomfort of diagnostic tools. The equipment is modern, and we saw a calmness in patients we had never seen before. The hospital sits in green, quiet surroundings, ideal for patients under stress. The whole institution gives off a vibe of healing that no amount of words can fully express.

Tailgating a trailblazer

A day with Yasas Hewage, banker turned pro-cyclist and founder of the Wroom community, Spinner Café and Sri Lanka’s first bike-fit lab.

By Svetlana De Fonseka AbeysekaraFirst published December 2015
▶ Watch
It’s like the principle of life. Work hard towards your goal. Have the guts to climb the hill. Yasas Hewage

Allowing a dream to take tangible form is often a risky affair, especially when that dream is one no one has dreamt before you. In pro-entrepreneurial Sri Lanka we are on the slow upward climb. As with any enterprise, the climb has been a challenge, the bumps rough and the journey long, perhaps more so in this venture. Dosage spent a day in the shoes of entrepreneur-extraordinaire Yasas Hewage, a marketer and banker by profession but an ardent cyclist first.

He is the founder of Spinner Café and Bike Fit Lab, which caters to a cycling-oriented audience, a first in the country. Yasas also founded Wroom, a community of cycling enthusiasts who meet in their mutual love of this unusual pastime, sharing ideas and gathering at first light to ride the island’s serpentine roads.

“Cycling-oriented” is not as limiting as one might think. Quite the opposite: anyone who steps into Spinner finds the embrace of this unlikely place endearing. You would be surprised how easily it sells the idea to strangers who drop into the café for a bite.

The Bike Fit Lab

The Spinner Bike Fit Lab is a novelty. From measuring visible attributes like your height to the reaction of your pulse, it uses patented technology to customise your bike for you. “The simple principle is that you can’t just go out and buy a bike and expect it to give you that experience,” Yasas says, showing us the racing bike at the centre of the lab. “Everyone’s body is different. Your heights might be the same, but your upper-body length, your arm and leg length may differ. Some of us have leg-length differences in the same body! The lab uses Retül bike-fit technology, the best in the world, to tailor a bike to your body geometry. In simple words, it’s like tailoring a suit.”

The lab performs a rigorous analysis of each rider using 3D motion-analysis technology. “First you need to understand the bike geometry,” says Yasas, presenting his racing bike. Sensors and cameras collect rider and bike data into a computer system, and, much like an ECG, a cyclist may do a motion analysis that monitors key parts of the anatomy while riding. “All these changes are implemented through a scientifically based system analysing the data. You can’t just perform them either. You need to be certified,” he says, having received his certification from Retül in Singapore.

The 5.30 am ride

Earlier that day, Dosage joined the Wroom family as they gathered outside Spinner at 5.30 am to catch the first rays of sun. After a few good hours of trailblazing through the dawn, the fifteen to twenty riders returned to Spinner Café for a hot cup of traditional green-herb soup (kola kenda) and some social banter.

“I joined the Wroom community in 2013,” says Sonali. “As a female cyclist, cycling in Sri Lanka is really difficult when you’re riding alone. Riding with a group gives me security, and the camaraderie of riding with people who think like I do. We want to enjoy the freedom of the road and the physical experience of getting your heart rate high and having a good time! There are a few more female cyclists now, and I hope I speak for them when I say Wroom has really improved my riding.”

Nishan, a young cyclist in the community, says: “I am a student of Yasas, studying CIM, and I used to sit in class listening to all these cycling stories and think, this guy is crazy! But then he inspired all of us. The twelve-hour, 280-kilometre ride to Trincomalee changed my life, because we realised the impossible could be done.”

The interview

Entrepreneur and pro-cyclist: you seem to have found your middle ground. Why cycling?

Four years ago I bought an affordable bike and decided to cycle cross-country, for fun. It grew into a bigger passion, and I wanted to bring more of an outdoor lifestyle into other people’s lives because I enjoyed it so much. We started the Wroom community in 2012, which was all about “wrooming” around on a bicycle, and it has grown into a fairly large community. On a personal level, I began performance cycling. Then, nine months ago, I left my day job (I worked for two banks over fifteen years) and put together the things I love, cycling and the café, and that gave birth to Spinner.

Do you prefer to be defined as a cyclist?

For the last eight years I have also run a marketing school, Strategy, with a friend. So I am a marketer by profession who worked in banking. But at some point you realise you can define who you are by your passion rather than what you do, and my passion is cycling, so I am undoubtedly a cyclist.

What exactly is the Wroom community?

Wroom was an idea. I started to enjoy cycling and thought it would be great to share. The philosophy that emerged is that Sri Lanka is growing on one side, but about ten percent of the country suffers from diabetes because of our lifestyle. We eat, we sleep, we get in our cars and watch a lot of television. We live in a country with beautiful weather 365 days a year, so why not energise Sri Lanka to enjoy the outdoors? Now people get in touch every day saying they want to take up cycling. We have members from sixteen to fifty. Cycling has become a fairly big industry, and it’s growing.

So Wroom is all about health and fitness?

We don’t promote the paranoid “I want to be healthy, I don’t want to eat” mentality. We all want our biscuit pudding. Our concept is simple: “Eat it and burn it.” We rode forty miles this morning; you get hungry, you have to eat. We have seen people come down from 150 kilos to 90, because it’s also fun. Wroom is for anyone. This morning there were people on their first ride, and others who have ridden 1,400 miles around the country. At the heart of Wroom is also road safety: our Blinking Lights project gives reflectors and lights to everyday commuters across the country.

Was Wroom born of Spinner, or Spinner of Wroom?

Wroom was the inspiration. “Spinning” is fundamental to cycling: it’s how you spin a pedal. (Usually the Sri Lankan spinner is a cricketer, he adds, laughing.) As long as you keep spinning, you can balance. Cycling early in the morning, there was no place to have a cup of coffee, so I started a café. And as cyclists we get obsessed with speed and improvement, so the bike fit came from that. Next we want to start a service centre, what we call a winkalé, which is a dying trade because no son wants to join the father’s business. But bike maintenance is highly technical, professional work, and we want to uplift it so more tech graduates come into it.

Do you have a daily cycling routine?

I race professionally, so I train seven days a week. That doesn’t mean riding hard every day: there are strength days and power days. The time I save not going to the bank, I spend on the bike.

What are the most gruelling challenges you’ve faced?

Climbing Kadugannawa for the first time was a big one; also Nuwara Eliya, Radella and Rabboda. My hobby is climbing. The ride to Nuwara Eliya was 152 kilometres non-stop, in 8 hours 52 minutes. On one off-road ride from Mullaitivu to Point Pedro, I fell and badly injured my hand, and had to ride one-handed for fourteen hours. I could have given up, but I didn’t. This bike has been around the country twice, and from Colombo to Arugam Bay in one day, in eleven hours. We’re running out of climbs in Sri Lanka, so in August we’re doing the Himalayas.

It’s a gruelling effort, but you find your glory at the top. Given the choice, I’d hit the hills.

Is the Yellow Jersey a goal?

For someone else, for sure. The Yellow Jersey at the Tour de France is the edge of pro-cycling, a decade of work. I would love to be part of someone else’s journey someday. To see a Sri Lankan get there would be amazing.

You are also a qualified cycling coach. What does that involve?

As a marketer I am a teacher, and I love teaching. When I wanted to improve my own cycling, there was no professional coach in the country, so I qualified through the British Cycling coaching programme. I hope to help more young competitive cyclists, and someday see a Sri Lankan on a Tour de France team. Endurance is something Sri Lankans have no excuse against: we are built for it. Our goal is to see that Olympic gold some day. Through one of our initiatives, the community also raised over twelve million rupees for a thousand wheelchairs for children with cerebral palsy. The community is pretty powerful.

Designing a rocket or a hospital?

Dr. Kithsiri Edirisinghe on why building a hospital is harder than sending a rocket to Mars.

The DosageFirst published December 2015
▶ Watch

In Sri Lanka, population growth is well below the replacement rate, which means a fast-ageing population alongside a high-risk young population. Older people tend to get more non-communicable diseases, while the young are at risk from drugs, alcohol and unprotected sex. Either way, there is great pressure on the healthcare system.

Dr. Kithsiri Edirisinghe is a medical administrator, an expert in hospital project management, and deputy chairman of the International Institute of Health Sciences (IIHS) in Welisara. According to Dr. Kithsiri, “Building a hospital is more difficult than sending a rocket to Mars.”

He told us there is only one hospital in Sri Lanka with no gate or boundary at its entrance. “A hospital needs to be open for anyone to come in. A hospital should be open 24/7, so gates are irrelevant.” The hospital is a place of healing and should be open to all.

The question is: why is it so difficult?

For a rocket, the slightest miscalculation can destroy the entire ship, but the maximum fatality would be ten or twelve. In a hospital where work is done on viruses, if a room isn’t properly sealed or ventilation is directed to the wrong place and a pathogen gets into the air, it would be a national disaster.

Building a hospital is more difficult than sending a rocket to Mars. Dr. Kithsiri Edirisinghe

The smallest detail matters. Administrators must take precautions against hospital-acquired infections, which can be averted by using materials that maintain a germ-free environment. Hospitals are meant to be a safe haven, so being germ-free should always be a top priority.

“There are many architectural monuments in this country. Even some hospitals are considered architectural monuments,” says Dr. Kithsiri. But regardless of interior or exterior design, the most important questions are: is the hospital universally accessible? Is it designed for people with disabilities? Is the environment calming? Is the building convenient and comfortable?

How important is a well-trained staff?

“When you’re building a hospital, the healthcare workers should be educated. If you’re designing a hospital, you have to professionally train nurses and medical administrators to run it smoothly and efficiently.” At IIHS, he says, they have already designed programmes with large industries that need healthcare workers. “We always try to match international standards. Because of the demand for nurses, it’s essential to train our students to be professional and excellent in their fields.”

Sex and Sri Lanka

Sexuality education from a psychological perspective, with Sarah Soysa, Dakshitha Wickremaratne and Nivendra Uduman.

The DosageFirst published December 2015
▶ Watch

Sarah Soysa

Social worker and educator

Speaking with Sarah Soysa was one of the easiest interviews we’ve done. On a taboo topic, her outgoing, cheerful personality helped. The topic? Sex education. Although it is said to be well covered in schools, there is still a large lack of openness in Sri Lanka, to the point that many are uneasy about sharing their problems “down there.”

A former member of the YWCA and a consultant at the FRIDA Young Feminist Fund, which supports feminist causes around the world, Sarah has vast experience in this field.

The first thing that came to light was that there is no formal curriculum for sex education. Most schools aim to cover it at some point, but there is no standard approach, which often makes the lesson ineffective, since the teacher has only their own idea of what you need to learn. On how it helps prevent abuse and violence, Sarah’s idea was systematic: it should start early. Even in year one, a child can learn the difference between “good touch, bad touch,” and information should grow with the child’s maturity, not with a set age.

On the internet, Sarah says there is more information available to this generation than ever, which leads to inaccurate information. Among peers who share the same sources, when enough people believe a lie, the lie becomes the truth.

When a large number of people believe the lie, the lie becomes the truth.

So how do you give the “birds and the bees” talk? It shouldn’t be tied to an age but to a level of maturity. Being able to talk to your child, or your parent, is the most important thing to establish. That kind of openness goes a long way, not only in overcoming issues but in bonding as a family. Sarah’s parting wisdom: have an open conversation, whether you are the parent or the child. Don’t judge, and be available to explore every avenue together. There is no rule about who has to start the conversation. Just ease into it.

Dakshitha Wickremaratne

Counselling psychologist

The biggest hurdle in sexuality education, says Dakshitha, is the generation gap. Teenagers getting “the talk” often feel embarrassed and awkward, and parents feel uneasy too, because that unease was ingrained in them at a young age. That almost always leads to an ineffective transfer of information.

Schools approach the subject in many ways, with varying success. Some leave it untouched and foster ignorance, and he often notices those are the schools where students are least open to discussing their problems and questions.

Another hindrance is the belief, among parents, teachers and policymakers, that if children learn about sex they will engage in it. The drawback is that children then find out by other means, which can lead to unsafe sex. In Dakshitha’s view, comprehensive sexual-health education ensures young people understand what is happening in their bodies and can make educated choices, because parents can’t be there in every situation. He recommends UNESCO’s guidance on sexuality education, which parents can use, especially if they are uncomfortable having the conversation themselves. Dakshitha travels the country delivering sexual-health workshops, and his passion and depth of knowledge are striking.

Nivendra Uduman

Counselling psychologist

How does a relationship work? What do you feel, think and, most importantly, do when faced with rejection? How do you manage the feelings and urges that come with sexual maturity? These should carry equal weight in sexuality education, says Nivendra Uduman, a counselling psychologist attached to the King’s College London resource centre for mental health in Colombo, who also practises privately. Teaching the mechanics of sex is no longer enough, he says; attitudes have changed drastically, and many no longer recognise the painful emotions that can follow in the wrong situation.

He takes a bold but educated stance on the taboo. In earlier centuries, he notes, people were more open about the human body: some of the greatest art, from Michelangelo’s David to Raphael’s Three Graces, depicts the nude. Over time, he argues, we have misread our own culture and heritage and turned mild amusement into shame. That is the taboo that exists today.

The notion this gives is that sex is a “dirty” thing to talk about, and it is often an avoided topic.

We have also mystified information about sex. Learning it only as “reproduction” in biology makes it seem wrong or undesirable to talk about, and psychologically gives the notion that it is “dirty.” The internet adds to the mystification, with misinformation that young people raised in our culture may take as normal.

So yes, there should be comprehensive sexuality education in schools, but the effort should start at home. Be open, and never show disgust, scorn or even discomfort, because those small signals go a long way in a child’s mind. If you are uncomfortable, books and factual programmes can help. The most important thing is to start, and to start as soon as possible.

We check every fact, figure and date against its original source before we publish. When a number comes from a report, we name the report. When we can’t confirm something, we leave it out.

We write our stories in our own words. When we rely on another outlet’s reporting, we say so in the story and link to it in the sources at the end. We never copy another publication’s work or present it as our own. Quotes are short, exactly as said or reported, and always credited.

Five or six years later

Eco-feminist, anarcho-primitivist

Elon Musk joked his new car could detach your retina. Could it?

Tesla says its flying Roadster will only get quicker, up to the limit of the human body. We checked what eye science says about that limit.

Explained · Health & ScienceEashaan Dassanayaka16 October 2026

Tesla has finally unveiled its second-generation Roadster, nine years after it was first promised. At a launch at SpaceX’s rocket test centre in McGregor, Texas, near Waco, on 15 October, the car hopped over a row of other vehicles on compressed-air thrusters and, in a live run, went from 0 to 96 km/h (60 mph) in 0.81 seconds.

Then Elon Musk said the production car would be even quicker. Citing Newton’s second law, he said more force and less mass would mean more acceleration. How much quicker? Tesla would, he said, “ride the limit of don’t detach the retina” of the person inside.

It was a joke. But it sets an unusual target: a car limited by the human body, not by its motor. So we looked at the science.

What 0.81 seconds does to you

Reaching 96 km/h in 0.81 seconds means an average push of about 3.4 g, more than three times your body weight pressing you back into the seat. Tesla says its top “Rocket” version will do it in under 0.59 seconds, a claim not yet shown in public. That works out to about 4.6 g on average, with brief peaks higher.

Mach 0.5Musk’s top-speed goal, about 617 km/h

Which way the force pushes matters most

Pilots and astronauts have been tested for decades, and the main lesson is that direction matters. When g-force runs from head to toe, as in a fighter jet’s tight turn, blood drains away from the eyes. NASA research found untrained people start losing side vision at about 4.1 g and black out at about 4.7 g.

A car launch pushes you from chest to back instead, and the body copes far better. NASA research puts the seated limit for sustained chest-to-back g at about 8 g, and the limiting factors are breathing and chest pain, not eyesight. In very short pulses, people have taken around 40 g for a quarter of a second.

Side note

Speed doesn’t hurt. Acceleration does. Just ask a fighter pilot.

The fastest crewed aircraft ever flown, NASA’s rocket-powered X-15, reached 4,520 mph (Mach 6.7) in 1967. The SR-71 Blackbird spy plane cruised at up to Mach 3.3. At a steady speed, though, the body feels nothing at all, which is why airline passengers can eat lunch at cruising speed. What strains the body, and the eyes, is changing speed or direction quickly.

That’s why fighter pilots worry most about g-force. An F-16 can pull up to 9 g in a hard turn, which tries to drain blood from the head. The first warning is “light loss”: vision dims and narrows before the pilot can pass out. Pilots fight it in three ways:

  1. A g-suit. Air bladders inflate around the stomach and legs. The US Air Force says the squeeze forces blood “up toward your cranium” to help keep vision and consciousness.
  2. The straining manoeuvre. Pilots tense their legs, buttocks and stomach, and take a quick breath about every three seconds, to keep blood pressure up in the head.
  3. A tilted seat. The F-16’s seat leans back 30 degrees instead of the usual 13, which the US Navy says improves g tolerance.

Notice that none of this is about the retina coming loose. For pilots, the danger to the eyes is blood leaving them. That’s the opposite direction to a car launch, which presses you back into your seat.

So can acceleration detach a retina?

The retina is the thin, light-sensing layer at the back of the eye. A detachment happens when it peels away from the tissue that supports it. It’s an emergency that can cause permanent vision loss.

Doctors have documented cases linked to acceleration, from roller coasters rather than cars. In 2024, eye specialists in Toronto reported two young women whose retinas detached after roller-coaster rides. Both already had risk factors: one had a family history of detachment, and the other was very short-sighted with weak areas of retina. Reviewing other reported cases, the authors concluded that a “structural predisposition is likely necessary.” In eyes without that weakness, similar forces caused bleeding, but not detachment. Two cases show a possible link, not proof, and they say nothing directly about a car.

Cases like these are rare. Answering a reader’s question for the American Academy of Ophthalmology, ophthalmologist Dr Richard Bensinger said roller coasters are “unlikely” to be a problem for short-sighted people.

The most extreme human test on record is US Air Force doctor John Paul Stapp’s. In 1954 he rode a rocket sled to 632 mph (about 1,017 km/h), then stopped in 1.4 seconds, taking 46.2 g. Almost all the tiny blood vessels in his eyes burst, and he feared his retinas had detached. He was temporarily blinded and left hospital the next day, but his eyesight was never quite the same. His was a sudden stop, not a launch, so it isn’t a safe or unsafe limit for a car; it shows how far human tests have gone.

Our verdict

No one has set a g-force at which a retina detaches. Force direction, how long it lasts and the state of the eye all matter. There is currently not enough evidence to show that the Roadster’s reported acceleration would cause retinal detachment in an otherwise healthy eye. For an eye that’s already vulnerable, the reported cases are rare, but they suggest a hard launch could matter, whether it comes from a roller coaster or a hypercar. So Musk’s joke points at a real risk, but the evidence doesn’t show it for most people at these speeds.

“The production car will actually do better than that.” Elon Musk, at the launch

Why it matters to you

  • Know your risk. Retinal detachment is more likely if you’re very short-sighted, have a family history of it, have had eye surgery such as cataract removal or a serious eye injury, or are aged about 40 to 70.
  • Before extreme rides such as launch roller coasters, theme-park thrill rides or track experiences, ask your eye doctor if any of those apply to you.
  • Know the warning signs: a sudden shower of floaters, flashes of light, blurred vision, or a curtain-like shadow over your vision. These need care right away. It’s painless, so don’t wait. In Australia, see your GP or optometrist the same day, or go to an emergency department. In Sri Lanka, go to the nearest hospital eye unit.
  • The car itself: Tesla is aiming to start production in 2028. It is taking reservations of US$50,000 to US$250,000, TechCrunch reported, and Musk said the car may not be legal in all places, so the thruster versions may never be road-legal in Australia or Sri Lanka.

Why food makes the difference

Each index is built from a “basket” of what households actually spend money on. Families outside Colombo spend a much bigger share of their money on food.

  • Food makes up about 26% of the Colombo basket.
  • Food makes up about 39% of the national basket.

So when rice, vegetables or coconut prices jump, the national figure tends to feel it more. When fuel, electricity, transport or rent rise, the Colombo figure can move faster. The dengue mosquito lays its eggs in small pools of clean water around homes, and it bites in the daytime. So the most useful habits are simple:

The cinnamon in your kitchen probably isn’t ours

Most cinnamon sold around the world is cassia. Ceylon cinnamon is a different spice, and Europe now protects its name.

FoodThe Dosage12 October 2026

Pick up a jar labelled “cinnamon” in a supermarket in Melbourne, London or Dubai, and there’s a good chance it isn’t what grows in Sri Lanka. It’s usually cassia: a close relative, grown mostly in China, Vietnam and Indonesia, that is cheaper, stronger and darker.

Two spices, one name

Ceylon cinnamon (Cinnamomum verum) is peeled from the inner bark and rolled by hand into thin, papery layers, like a cigar. It’s light tan, crumbles easily, and tastes sweet and delicate. Cassia is a single thick, hard curl of bark: darker, harsher and spicier.

~100×more coumarin in cassia than in Ceylon cinnamon
6 mgsafe daily coumarin limit for a 60 kg adult
~2 gof the strongest cassia tested reaches that limit
2022the year the EU protected the name “Ceylon cinnamon”

Why it matters for your health

Cassia contains a natural compound called coumarin, which in large amounts can harm the liver. Europe’s food safety authority sets a tolerable limit of 0.1 mg per kilo of body weight per day, which is 6 mg for a 60 kg adult. Austria’s food agency measured cassia at 1,880 to 3,260 mg of coumarin per kilo, and the highest Ceylon sample at 185 mg. By our calculation, about 2 grams of the strongest cassia, less than a teaspoon, reaches the daily limit. You’d need more than 30 grams of Ceylon cinnamon to do the same.

Same name on the jar. A hundred times the coumarin.

A name worth protecting

In February 2022, the European Union recognised Ceylon cinnamon as a protected geographical indication, the first for any Sri Lankan product. Only cinnamon grown and processed here, the traditional way, can be sold in the EU under that name. Mexico, where cinnamon goes into churros, hot chocolate and café de olla, is another loyal buyer: cinnamon tops the list of what Sri Lanka sells there.

How to tell at home

Look for “Ceylon cinnamon” or Cinnamomum verum on the label. Check the sticks: many thin layers rolled together means Ceylon; one thick hollow tube means cassia. And if you’re in Sri Lanka, you’re already in the right place.

The two-minute walk after dinner

A short stroll after eating blunts the blood sugar spike. You don’t need a gym, or even ten minutes.

After a plate of rice and curry, the natural move is the sofa. A growing body of research suggests getting up for a few minutes instead is one of the simplest things you can do for your blood sugar.

2–5minutes of light walking per break in the studies
17%lower blood sugar with light walking, compared with sitting
9.5%lower with standing breaks alone
60–90minutes after eating, when blood sugar tends to peak

What the research found

In 2022, researchers at the University of Limerick pulled together seven studies comparing three things: sitting for long stretches, breaking that up by standing, and breaking it up with light walking. Participants took short breaks of two to five minutes, every 20 to 30 minutes across the day.

Standing helped: blood sugar was about 9.5% lower on average than with prolonged sitting. Light walking helped more, at about 17% lower, and blood sugar and insulin also rose and fell more gently. The walking was not exercise in the gym sense. It was a stroll.

Not a workout. A stroll.

Because blood sugar tends to peak within an hour or so of a meal, the best time to move is soon after eating. Walk to the end of the lane and back. Do a few laps of the garden. Take the stairs. Wash up standing, then take your phone call on foot. At the office, a two-minute walk every half hour does the same job.

It’s not a replacement for medication or a doctor’s advice, especially if you have diabetes. But it’s free, it takes two minutes, and you can start tonight.

The dollar jar

Sri Lanka buys fuel, medicine and many foods from abroad, and the sellers want dollars. So the country keeps a jar of them: its foreign reserves. Tourism, exports and money sent home by Sri Lankans abroad fill the jar. Repaying foreign loans empties it.

In April 2022 Sri Lanka told its foreign lenders it would stop repaying them for now. That is a default. Picture a family announcing at a wedding that it can't pay back any relative this year: everyone hears, and nobody lends again until there's a plan.

A default isn't a technicality. It's the moment the whole world stops lending.
